ARIMA: A Simple Guide to Time Series Forecasting
=================================================

WHAT IS ARIMA?
--------------
ARIMA stands for AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average. 

It is a classic statistical model used to predict future points in a time series (data collected over regular intervals, such as daily stock prices, monthly sales, or hourly temperature).

Think of ARIMA as an intelligent trend analyst that looks at past values and past prediction errors to guess what happens next.


THE 3 PARTS OF ARIMA (p, d, q)
-------------------------------
An ARIMA model is described by three numbers: ARIMA(p, d, q).

1. AR (AutoRegressive) -> Parameter 'p'
   - Concept: "The past predicts the future."
   - Meaning: It uses past values of the data to predict current values.
   - Analogy: If yesterday and the day before were hot, today will likely be hot too.
   - 'p' represents how many past time periods (lags) the model looks back at.

2. I (Integrated) -> Parameter 'd'
   - Concept: "Making the data steady."
   - Meaning: It subtracts current values from previous values to remove trends or seasonality (a process called "differencing").
   - Why it matters: Time series models work best on "stationary" data (data whose average value doesn't drift upward or downward over time).
   - 'd' represents how many times you have to difference the data to make it flat/steady.

3. MA (Moving Average) -> Parameter 'q'
   - Concept: "Learning from past mistakes."
   - Meaning: It looks at the errors (shocks or unexpected spikes) from past predictions and uses them to correct future predictions.
   - Analogy: If an unexpected heatwave made your prediction off yesterday, you adjust today's expectation based on that error.
   - 'q' represents how many past prediction errors the model factors in.


REAL-WORLD EXAMPLE: DAILY ICE CREAM SALES
------------------------------------------
Imagine you run an ice cream shop and want to forecast tomorrow's sales.

* AR part (p=1): Looks at yesterday's sales. (High sales yesterday suggest high sales today.)
* I part (d=1): Account for the summer trend where sales are rising daily by subtracting yesterday's total from today's total to focus on relative growth.
* MA part (q=1): Adjusts for unexpected events. (If an unannounced street festival caused a spike in sales yesterday, the model accounts for that surprise error when predicting today.)


PROS AND CONS OF ARIMA
-----------------------
Pros:
+ Very effective for short-term forecasting.
+ Requires only historical time-series data (no need for complex extra features).
+ Grounded in solid, reliable statistical theory.

Cons:
- Performs poorly on complex, highly non-linear data.
- Struggles with long-term predictions (errors compound over time).
- Basic ARIMA cannot automatically handle seasonal patterns (you need SARIMA for data with strong repeating cycles like monthly retail sales).


WHEN TO USE ARIMA?
------------------
- Predicting stock market indices or exchange rates (short-term).
- Forecasting energy demand, web traffic, or product sales over days or weeks.
- Any time-ordered dataset that exhibits clear historical patterns or trends.
